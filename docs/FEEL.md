# How our products must feel

Every feature in this repo is built to this standard. An agent reads this before designing anything the
user can see or touch. It is not decoration: it is the difference between a tool people abandon and one
they come back to.

## The mechanism, honestly

Anticipation drives return, not the reward itself. Dopamine rises on the *expectation* of a reward and on
the *variability* of it, which is why a predictable prize stops working after a week. So:

- **Vary the size of a reward, never the certainty of it.** The player always gets something for finishing;
  how good it is varies. Never make a promised reward a coin flip, and never use a paid loot box.
- **Show the reward coming before it lands.** A chest that appears, fills and then opens beats a number that
  silently increases. The two seconds of anticipation are the point.
- **Near miss beats easy win.** Royal Match, the top grossing match-3, tunes levels so players often fail by
  one or two moves, and that "almost" is what pulls them back in. Apply it honestly: tune difficulty so the
  player *genuinely* nearly succeeded. Never fake a near miss by stealing a win they earned.

## Response: the first 100 milliseconds

- Every tap answers within 100ms with something visible, even if the real work takes longer. A pressed state,
  a ripple, a number moving. Silence reads as broken.
- Nothing important ever waits on the network. Act locally, reconcile after.
- Numbers count up, they never jump. Bars fill, they never snap.

## Progress must be visible at all times

- The player can always see where they are and what is next: a ring, a bar, a step count, a streak.
- Progress moves immediately when they act, in the same frame as the tap.
- Never show a goal without showing distance to it.

## Completion: earn the celebration

- Finishing something gets a real moment: motion, sound, a figure that lands with weight. Scale it to the
  achievement, so a daily goal is not the same fanfare as a 30 day streak.
- End a session on a high. If the player is about to leave, let the last thing they see be a win, a
  milestone, or what is waiting tomorrow.

## What we will not do

These are not style preferences. They are the line between engaging and exploitative, and crossing it costs
ratings, reviews and eventually the listing.

- **No punishing timers.** No lives, no energy walls, no waiting to play.
- **No fake urgency.** No invented countdowns, no "3 people are looking at this", no manufactured scarcity.
- **No loss framing.** Never guilt someone for a broken streak or an absence. Offer a restore, once, quietly.
- **No interruption.** Ads and prompts never land mid-action. Rewarded ads are opt in and clearly labelled.
- **No dark patterns.** The way out of a screen is as obvious as the way in.
- **Respect the body.** Everything above has a reduced-motion path that keeps the information and drops the
  movement. Sound and haptics are opt out. Nothing relies on colour alone.

## Craft that carries the feeling

- Motion has physics: spring easing, overshoot on arrival, weight that matches the object's size.
- Sound is short, generated in code, and pitched to the moment. It layers with combos rather than repeating.
- Haptics mark the moment of impact, never the whole animation.
- Type carries hierarchy before colour does. Numbers that align use tabular figures.
- Every asset is generated by us or properly licensed, so nothing can be claimed against us after publishing.

## How to use this file

Before building a user-facing feature, name which of these the feature uses: anticipation, variable reward,
visible progress, or completion. If it uses none, it is a utility, and that is fine. If it uses one, build
it to the standard above. If it breaks a "will not do" rule, do not build it; raise the conflict instead.
